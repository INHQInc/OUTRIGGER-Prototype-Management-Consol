Outrigger · home page · five ideas

Five doors from looking to booking

The experiment proved we can move attention. It also proved the page has nowhere for that attention to go. Five ways to build the exit — ranked, each with the evidence behind it.

The finding everything below is built on.

Over eighteen days the new hero moved 286 clicks off the offer. Every one reappeared in a browse action — destination tabs +82, the Explore menu +80, property titles +62, the dropdown +52. And not a single commerce action moved: property “Book Now” gained one click, Offer CTA gained one, Rooms & Rates fell 21.

The guests who did reach the booking engine converted far better — 28.9% → 34.4%. The filter works. There is just no door into it.

What your reference board already says

Twenty-five collected home pages, read against our own data

The gallery is overwhelmingly one thing: editorial, destination-led, image-first — Aman, One&Only, Peninsula, Vignette, Belmond. Soft single lines (“Memories Well Made”, “Your One&Only escape, your way”, “Begin your journey”), destination carousels, property grids with almost no copy, offers present but subordinate, stories at the bottom.

That board is a strong argument that v1's direction was right. It is also, almost entirely, a collection of styles — and our problem is not style, it's mechanism. With one exception worth crediting: several of the references put a search directly beneath an editorial hero and lose nothing of the mood for it. That pattern is the answer to idea one, and it is already on your board.

The five

Ranked by expected effect on Rooms & Rates arrivals

1

The Plan Bar — ask once, price everything

An island and a rough month captured in the hero, which then follows the guest down the page.

The hero currently spends its only click on Learn more. Replace that with one soft question — island chips and a month, no date grid, no guest count. One tap and a month.

The answer then docks as a slim persistent bar and makes every surface below it priceable: destination cards show a from-rate, property tiles show real availability, offers re-render as your rate rather than a generic percentage. This is the foundation the other four stand on — nothing else on this list works as well without it.

Why this, from our data

The hero proved it can command attention — 286 clicks redirected. Nothing on the page currently captures that attention as intent. Reaching a rate today requires supplying dates at the end of a long browse; capturing them at the start makes every later click a rates click.

Risk

It's the most conventional move in hotel web design and v1 was about escaping convention. Resolve it on the distinction: a rate for your dates is utility; a percentage off is a promise. Keep the imagery and the experience-led line, and let the question sit underneath rather than replace it — which is exactly what the references on your board do.

2

Choose the room, not the resort

Invert the taxonomy. Guests have opinions about ocean-front and steps-to-the-beach. They have none about Reef versus Beachcomber.

The home page asks guests to pick a property — a brand abstraction a first-time visitor cannot hold an opinion about. Replace the primary choice with the thing they actually want: ocean-front room · steps to the sand · suite with a lanai · adults-only quiet · family connecting rooms. Pick the want, and the page returns which properties have it, on your dates, from a price.

This is the most dramatic idea on the list and I think the most valuable, because it removes a decision the guest is not equipped to make and replaces it with one they made before they arrived. It also fits the brand's actual promise — the hero is selling a view, and then the page asks you to choose a hotel.

Why this, from our data

Property titles +11.3% — they open properties. Property “Learn More” −5.9% — they don't want the brochure. Property “Book Now” −0.7% — they won't commit. They are opening properties to compare, and the page gives them nothing to compare on.

Risk

It fights the property-brand hierarchy and every stakeholder who owns a property will feel demoted. It's also the biggest content lift — every room needs attribute tagging. Worth piloting on one island before the estate.

3

Live rates on every browse surface

If a card is worth clicking, it's worth pricing. Destination cards and property tiles carry a real from-rate.

Every exploration surface on the page today ends in a content CTA: Oahu highlights, Learn More, View more stories. Put a live from-rate and an availability signal on the destination cards and the property tiles, and give each a single action — “See rates”, replacing the Book Now / Learn More pair guests are currently refusing both of.

Pair it with the Plan Bar and the rate is for their month, which is the difference between a price and a quote.

Why this, from our data

Destination tabs +8.3% and dropdown +12.7% are the biggest gains on the page — and the explorer's own exit button, Destination Highlights, is −7.0%. Guests are opening the explorer and refusing what it offers them.

Risk

Rates on a brand page can read as a discount page — the exact framing v1 removed. Show a from-rate, never a strike-through or a percentage. And it needs a live rate feed on a cached marketing page, which is real engineering.

4

Flexible dates first — “sometime in March”

A price-by-month calendar, so a guest can enter the funnel before they've decided anything.

The booking engine demands exact dates. Most home page visitors don't have them — they have a season and a feeling. Offer a month view with the cheapest nights surfaced, let them enter on “first week of March, four nights”, and firm it up inside the engine.

This is the single biggest commitment barrier on the path, and it sits right at the point our data shows guests stopping.

Why this, from our data

Rooms & Rates arrivals −4.9% while every upstream browse metric rose. The guests are there and qualified — arrivals convert at 34.4% — but the last step asks for a precision they don't have yet.

Risk

Flexible search is expensive to compute across an estate, and a cheapest-night view can pull demand into low-rate dates you'd rather not discount. Cap it at a from-rate per week rather than a full price grid.

New feature
5

The hero you can tap — the view becomes the room

Desire is formed by the image. Make the image the entry point.

The hero already shows the thing being sold: a view. Today it's a poster you can only Learn more about. Put quiet hotspots on it — “this is the view from an Ocean Front King at OUTRIGGER Reef — see it from $X” — so the shortest possible path exists between wanting what you're looking at and pricing it.

Not one of the twenty-five references on your board does this. They all treat the hero as a poster. It uses assets you already own, it is unmistakably premium rather than transactional, and it converts the most-viewed pixel on the site into a commerce surface without putting a single price in the headline.

Why this, from our data

Scroll depth is flat to negative at all four levels — nothing new below the fold will be seen. Any new idea has to earn its place in the first viewport, and the hero is the only real estate that qualifies.

Risk

Interactive heroes age badly and can feel gimmicky if overdone. One or two hotspots, revealed on hover or after a beat, never a busy overlay. Also the highest production cost per destination — shoot once, reuse hard.

All five share one primary metric: Visit Page: Booking Engine: Rooms & Rates, baseline 4.27%. At the traffic v1 accrued — 12,804 per arm in 18 days — a 20% move resolves in about 14 days. Guardrails carry over from v1: hero CTA must stay down, destination explorer and property tile engagement must not fall back, offer engagement must not fall, and bookings-per-arrival must hold at 34.4% — volume must not cost quality.

What I would not spend a test on.

More inspirational content. Scroll is flat to negative at every depth; it would be served to the same eyeballs. Another hero copy test — −58.1% at p<0.0001 is as settled as this programme gets. Returning to offer-led framing — offer engagement was flat when the offer left the hero, which means offer demand is inelastic to it and that headline was never earning its place.
