Outrigger · home page · Verndale review

Verndale, prioritised

Every proposal from the content audit and the page annotations, ranked against what the hero experiment actually measured — with a modified approach wherever the evidence points somewhere better.

The short version

Where an eighteen-day experiment and a content audit landed in the same place

Verndale's central diagnosis and the experiment's are the same finding.

“The homepage should move from being a collection of promotions, destinations, stories and selling points to becoming a guided journey from inspiration to action.”

The experiment measured that gap rather than describing it. The new hero moved 286 clicks out of the offer and into browsing — and property “Book Now” gained one click in eighteen days, the offer CTA gained one, and Rooms & Rates fell 21. Inspiration works. The journey to action doesn't exist.

And their most important constraint is the one the data proves hardest.

“The recommendation is not to add more content. It is to give every part of the homepage a defined role within the customer journey and remove or deprioritize content that does not support that journey.”

Scroll depth is flat to negative at all four levels — −0.3%, −0.7%, −0.9%, −2.6%. Anything added below the fold is served to the same eyeballs. Two independent methods, same answer: this page needs subtraction.

Which is why the two annotations proposing new sections are the two I'd modify hardest — they contradict Verndale's own principle.

What the audit can't see is mechanism, not clarity.

Nearly every annotation is right on its own terms, and two are confirmed hard by the data. What a content audit cannot reach is what happens when a guest presses the thing.

Three of the four routes into booking — property tiles, header, sticky bar — open the on-site widget and force an exact date before the guest has chosen a property. The fourth, the offer tiles, links straight out with no dates and a destination parameter SynXis discards, landing on a worldwide list. Opposite mechanisms, identical outcome: both flat. Neither serves a guest who is still choosing.

That is the largest finding on this page, no amount of better copy on those buttons fixes it, and it is why the top two items in the table below are not in the audit.

Updated 19 Aug — five decisions have changed this ranking.

  • “Carry dates into the engine” is dropped. I had the mechanism wrong: property tiles, header and sticky bar all open the on-site widget, which already forces date selection. The capture exists. Only the offer tiles link out dateless.
  • Every home page CTA now routes through the widget (decided). Which makes softening the widget's date default the largest single lever on the page — it sits in front of 100% of booking intent.
  • The explorer is deferred — too complicated for now, not disproven.
  • Offer clicks should ask for a property first, then dates. New lead candidate.
  • Vacation type becomes a Trip Planner banner under the explorer.

And one reading now carries most of the weight.

Same card, same guests, two paths: Property Title — an ungated link — +11.3%, against Book Now — which opens a date-gated widget — one click in eighteen days. Guests take the ungated path by an order of magnitude.

Cheap commitments first, expensive ones later. That single sentence now decides most of what follows, including why “Explore Dates” is the wrong verb and why an offer click should ask for a property before a date.

Two blockers to clear.

The widget cannot pass a promo code, and is meant to. A defect. Any offer route that depends on the widget carrying promo is blocked until it's fixed — otherwise offer-branded cards show rack rates. #1 sidesteps this: the promo rides the property choice into the existing direct link.

The Trip Planner is the favourites feature, not a vacation-type chooser. Saving is not booking — and guests take the cheapest commitment available, so a save button directly under the explorer may divert people who would otherwise have reached rates. Test it with Rooms & Rates arrivals as primary, which must not fall; or show it conditionally, after two exploration actions with no rates click, which removes the risk rather than measuring it. And settle first: promote favourites, or build a vacation-type chooser?

On Homepage 2.0 itself. Verndale's seven-section structure — Promise, Choose Your Journey, Why Outrigger, Find the Place That Fits, Experience the Destination, Make the Trip Possible, Book with Confidence, Continue the Journey — is a destination, not a test. You can't A/B a rebuild. Treat it as the target state and let each test above move the page toward it: P2 and P4 together are “Find the Place That Fits”; P1 and P3 are “Make the Trip Possible”; P5 is “Book with Confidence”. The structure earns its keep as a scoring rubric for what to build next, not as a launch.

Each proposal, with the evidence

What they said · what the data says · what I'd change

P1

Carry the guest's dates into the booking engine

Not in the audit — from the experiment

The evidence

Property “Book Now” moved from 294 → 295 clicks in eighteen days. Offer CTA, 198 → 199. Both reach the booking engine already. Neither converts, because both land on an empty search form.

I verified the engine honours dates: reservation.outrigger.com/?chain=18497&arrive=…&depart=… applies them and lands on the rated “Select a Hotel” grid. And every property in the page's own JSON carries a PropertySabreID, so each card can build its own dated link.

What to build

Capture island and rough month once. Append arrive/depart to every existing booking link. No new component, no rate feed, no CMS change.

P2

“Birds-eye view of where your properties are”

Annotation 3 — and “I also missed the drop down at first”

“Would love ability to view all your locations for a birds-eye-view of where your properties [are] (I also missed the drop down at first).”

The evidence

The destination surfaces are the biggest gainers on the page — tabs +8.3%, dropdown +12.7% — and the explorer's own exit button, Destination Highlights, is −7.0%. Guests open it in growing numbers and refuse what it offers. Verndale's “I missed the dropdown” is a discoverability complaint about the single best-performing control on the page.

Modified approach

Not a map section — a two-view explorer replacing the current selector. Grid for inspiration, Map (with the list as its left pane) for decision, sharing one zoom so the list is always “what's in view”. Their three headline suggestions all work; Pick Your Paradise is the one that isn't generic.

Absorb the dropdown rather than replace it — it's your biggest gainer, and drilling is for browsers while the dropdown serves people who already know.

P3

“Book Now may feel too aggressive on the home page”

Annotation 4

Test softer CTA variations: “Explore Dates”, “View Options”, “Plan Your Stay” · progressive CTAs, softer on homepage → stronger on property page · personalisation, returning visitors see “Book Now” · microcopy such as “No payment required”. And: “Explore Property” or “View Property” instead of “Learn more”.

The evidence

Correct, and the number is brutal: one extra click in eighteen days. Meanwhile “Learn More” fell −5.9% while the property name rose +11.3%. Guests want the place — not the brochure, and not the commitment.

Modified approach

  • Delete a CTA, don't rename both. Two competing actions and guests take neither. One action per card. “Explore Dates” is the best of their suggestions because it names what actually happens next.
  • Softening the verb alone won't fix it. The destination is a blank form. Pair this with P1 or it's a nicer-sounding door into the same waiting room.
  • Their microcopy idea is the cheap version of P5 — “No payment required” under the CTA does the book-direct reassurance job without spending a section on it.
P4

“Search by vacation types rather than location”

Annotation 7 — adventure · wellness · romance · family

The evidence

No direct read from the experiment, but it aligns with the audit's own second theme — “help travelers choose with confidence” — and with the strongest behavioural signal we have: guests open properties to compare them (+11.3%) and the page gives them nothing to compare on. A first-timer can't rank Kaua'i against Kona; they can absolutely say “romantic” or “with kids”.

Modified approach

A second axis on the explorer, not a separate feature. Where and what kind, filtering one result set. Building it as its own section adds page length the scroll data says nobody will reach.

The tagging is the real cost. Note the property JSON ships PropertyHighlights.Amenities as an empty array — but the property pages carry full amenity lists, so the raw material exists and needs harvesting plus roughly twenty synonym groups to collapse “Swimming pool” and “Outdoor pool & hot tub” into one key.

P5

A book-direct value block

Annotation 5 — with the Marriott Bonvoy example

“Would benefit from a headline/section that promotes the value of booking direct for people who come here to ‘validate’ site and then book on third-party sites.”

The evidence — mixed, and worth reading carefully

Offer engagement was flat when the offer left the hero (+2.2%, p=0.86). Demand for rate messaging on this page is inelastic — more of it did not produce more clicks. That argues against another rate-flavoured block.

But their hypothesis is different from ours: it's about leakage to OTAs, not about discount appetite. That's genuinely untested, and it's the audit's fifth strategic theme. It deserves a run — just not on the assumption that offer messaging is under-supplied here.

Modified approach

Test the microcopy version first — their own annotation 4 suggestion, a line under the CTA. It costs no scroll depth, no new section, and isolates the reassurance effect. If it moves, then fund the block. Measure it on Rooms & Rates arrivals, never on offer clicks.

P6

“Signature Experiences Start Here”

Annotation 6

“Would love to add a headline/section to reinforce authentic experiences… And showcase other examples since Cirque de Soleil ‘Auana is only at Waikiki.”

The evidence — against the vehicle, not the idea

Adding a section contradicts Verndale's own principle (“the recommendation is not to add more content”) and the scroll data rules it out independently: flat to negative at all four depths. The neighbouring Cirque block already earns almost nothing, and Story clicks sit at 0.46%.

Modified approach

Put the signature experience on the property, not in a section. The differentiators are already in the amenity data and they're excellent — Monkeypod Kitchen at Reef, Duke's Waikiki and Blue Note Hawaii at Waikiki Beach, 'Auana at Beachcomber, Appetito at Paradise, Holokai Catamaran. Surface them as the computed second line on each card in the explorer's list.

That answers their own follow-on point exactly — it showcases what's distinctive at every property instead of one — and it costs no page length.

P7

Rename “Discover paradise”

Annotation 7 — “Travel Inspiration” or “Vacation Ideas”

The evidence

The critique is fair — the label doesn't set expectations for blog content. But the block earns 0.46% and is falling (−8.8%). Renaming the lowest-earning section on the page has a ceiling of roughly nothing.

Modified approach

Don't rename it — repurpose the slot. It's ten story links occupying the largest block on a page only a quarter of visitors reach the bottom of. Put the vacation-type chooser (P4) there. Verndale's two halves of annotation 7 solve each other: the weakest real estate on the page becomes the home of their strongest idea.

P8

Value prop & the five brand pillars

Annotation 2

The evidence — none yet, and that's the problem

Renowned beach locations, the must-see beach bar, live music, signature experiences, conservation. Strong brand material and it maps to Homepage 2.0's “Why Outrigger”. But it sits on the one piece of ground where we have a settled result: hero CTA −58.1%, p<0.0001. Changing the area around a proven win risks giving it back.

Modified approach

Run it only after P1–P3, and pre-register Hero CTA Click must stay down as a hard guardrail. If offer clicks rebound, the pillars have reopened the door the hero test closed.

P9

Gift cards and email signup copy

Annotation 8 (both)

The evidence

Gift Card clicks: 0.21%, −1.1% — among the smallest numbers on the page. Their questions are good ones (does it expire, does it work at any hotel) and the email critique is sound.

Modified approach

Fix them, don't test them. These are content corrections worth making because they're right, not experiments worth spending two weeks of traffic on. Email signup in particular serves a different goal with a different metric — it shouldn't compete for home page test capacity aimed at bookings.

The sequence I'd run

Each ~14 days at current traffic to resolve a 20% move

  1. P1 — dates into the engine. Cheapest, strongest evidence, and it's the prerequisite that makes P2 and P3 worth measuring. Also the only one nobody has proposed.
  2. P3 — one CTA per card, “Explore Dates”. Trivial build, rides on P1, directly answers the one-click-in-eighteen-days finding.
  3. P2 — the explorer. Grid + Map, replacing the destination selector, exits carrying dates. Your map prototype is most of the work already.
  4. P4 — vacation type as the explorer's second axis, in the “Discover paradise” slot.
  5. P5 — book-direct microcopy, then the block only if it moves.

P6 folds into P2 as card content. P7 is absorbed by P4. P8 waits until the hero result is banked. P9 is a content ticket.

One thing worth saying to Verndale.

The audit is strong on clarity — what each block says, whether it sets expectations, whether the language matches intent. Nearly every annotation is right on its own terms. Where it doesn't reach is mechanism: what actually happens when a guest presses the thing. The single largest finding on this page is that four separate routes into the booking engine all arrive without dates. No amount of better copy on those buttons fixes it, and no content audit would have found it.

Worth pairing their next round with the experiment data directly — their instincts were right on “Book Now” and on the birds-eye view, and the numbers would have told them how right, and why.
